The previous post ended flat. One lever was left: treat different items differently. Fine-tune per group, cross-learn inside a group, one model per group.

That lever has a precondition, and it is testable beforhand. For a static grouping to help, difficulty has to be a stable property of an item. If the items that are hard this week are not the ones that were hard last week, it means the error cannot be decreased by finding a different setup for the worst items, as it is akin to a transient effect.

This post answers that, and then keeps narrowing. Difficulty is a stable property of the item. Two thirds of it is volume, which needs no clustering to exploit.

The panel

To analyse the difficulty’s, we divide the time horizon in twenty-four origins, one week apart, in order to score over 7-day non-overlapping windows (non-overlapping matters: with overlapping windows the correlation between consecutive origins would be partly an artefact of shared days). The restricted model used in this post is the zero-shot one at the context length chosen for days 1–7 (see the previous post), predicting 7 days ahead only — the fine-tuned model is disqualified here, because walking its test-origin checkpoint backwards would let it see the future of every earlier window.

The dataset sample is the same as in the previous post, and amongst the 494 series that are scorable at least once, 138 are scorable at every origin, so they constitute our panel. This way, every item in the panel has its difficulty measured on the same set of weeks. The figure below plots each of the selected item’s relative difficulty at each forecast origin:

Though we can suspect an at least mild correlation between weeks, as the graph suggests some horizontal lines, the error ditribution and origin needs to be properly assessed. So we split the error of each (item, origin), written \(x_{i,o}\), cell in four ways:

\[x_{i,o} = \mu + \alpha_i + \beta_o + \varepsilon_{i,o}\]

where \(\mu\) is the global mean; \(\alpha_i\) is the item — by construction the only part a static grouping could exploit; \(\beta_o\) is the week - are some weeks are hard for everyone? What is left splits further into a part that survives to the next origin (persistent), a part that does not (transient), and the sampling noise of the cell itself, measured from the odd and even days of each window. It is all contained within \(\varepsilon_{i,o}\).

Our task now is to scan the distribution for clusters and continuums.

Does the estimator work?

Before touching the real panel, I ran our scaning method on a simulated one with known components, at the same panel size and noise level. The results are:

The left subfigure proves we can trust the method for recovering error shares. However, the right subfigure is the method’s one real limit. In case of a slow drift, it is hard to tell from a constant difficulty over six months, and the estimator divides it badly: at an autocorrelation of 0.97, a drift with no constant item effect planted anywhere comes back as a 16% item effect. The total it does get right, to within 1.4 points everywhere on the grid.

The takeaway is that the item share answers could a grouping fitted once and left alone help, and the sum answers is there anything stable at all, even if it has to be refitted.

Two numbers summarise the section.

  1. The identity of the item accounts for 42% of the variance in log WQL once the noise inside each \(\alpha_i\) is removed.
  2. the week accounts for 2% — there are essentially no weeks that are hard for everybody. Difficulty mostly comes from the item.

Most of what remains is sampling noise, which is what forecasting 7-day windows of items selling a few units a week buys you. That matters for the next section, in which we try to findt he same result with a different tool.

Two instruments, one answer

The previous section gave us our conclusion. But a decomposition is a model, and a model can be wrong. Thereofer, we may want to use something like rank agreement to confirm our findings: it is a direct measurement of consistency, with no assumptions behind it:

Immediately, the median of +0.52 between consecutive origins looks like a weak agreement, challenging the previous section’s result (test–retest conventions call anything under 0.5 weak). Taken at face value, the two sections seem to disagree: a large item effect, and a ranking that only half holds.

But they don’t. The decomposition predicts the rank correlation, and this prediction can be written down from the variance shares alone:

  1. Within one origin the week effect is a constant and does not touch the across-items ranking, so the spread of the measured error at a single origin is \(\operatorname{V}_{cell} = \operatorname{var}(\alpha) + v_{\text{pers}} + v_{\text{trans}} + v_{\text{noise}}\)
  2. The covariance between two consecutive origins keeps only the parts that survive the step from the previous value, \(\operatorname{var}(\alpha) + v_{\text{pers}}\).
  3. Their ratio is the correlation the model expects anyone to observe. Six months apart the persistent part has decayed and only \(\operatorname{var}(\alpha)\) remains.
  4. So rank correlation is equal to \(\frac{\operatorname{var}(\alpha)}{\operatorname{V}_{cell}}\).

Therefore noise enters only in the denominator, and being responsible for around 40% of cell’s error, so it halves this correlation rate, despite being unrepresentative of the actual phenomenon.

So divide instead by the reliable part of a cell — everything except the sampling noise — and what comes out is the correlation between the underlying difficulties rather than between their noisy estimates:

The figure shows the difference between the original blunt ruler and the more faithfull one: the grey bar is what the bluntness costs, and the underlying difficulty ordering is stable at 0.86 - confirming the previous section’s strong conclusion.

The residuals are reassuring (right pannel): they fall on opposite sides of zero. A systematic error in the variance model would push both the same way.

The six-month row says something further. Nearly all of the long-run agreement is the \(\alpha\) term — the persistent part has decayed by then — so \(\alpha\) is behaving like a genuinely fixed property of the item rather than a slow drift. If we remember section “Does the estimator work?”’s warning, we see that we are safe.

Most of it is volume

A large item share isn’t the end of our analysis. WQL is scale-free, so a dependence on volume would not be an artefact of units — it would be a statement about predictability: “an item selling a third of a unit a day has an irreducibly high relative error, and that is a permanent property of the item”.

That distinction decides what \(\alpha_i\) is worth. If it is a function of volume, then “group the items by difficulty” means “bucket them by volume”, which needs no clustering and which the forecaster reads off the history unaided.

One correction is mandatory though. The sampling noise of a 7-day window is itself strongly volume-dependent, so low-volume items have their \(\alpha\) inflated more — which would manufacture exactly the relationship I am testing for. A global bias adjustment cannot fix a bias that differs per item, so I measure each item’s noise from its own odd and even days and remove it individually.

So we obtain a good linear tendency that confirms the initial statement: volume and sparsity together explain 63% of the stable difficulty. Two details matter for reading that.

They are not two different readings. Volume alone reaches the same 63%, soadding the zero-day share moves nothing. At a Spearman of +0.71 with \(\alpha\) and a near-mechanical relationship to volume on data that is two thirds zeros, sparsity is a second view of the same quantity, not an independent factor.

And the noise channel is not what is driving it. Wwe already justified that a noisier estimate of the same underlying error could have a different expectation. The direct test is to ask how much of \(\alpha\) the measurement noise itself explains: it reaches 0.63 as a Spearman against 0.77 for volume, and 34% of the variance against 63%. If the volume relationship were an artefact of the noise level, the noise level would be the proximate cause and the stronger correlate. It is the weaker one, by a clear margin.

So two thirds of the stable item effect is a sort on a number already in the history, and one third is not: 15.6% of the whole error budget, against 2.0% for the week and 11.5% for everything transient. It is the largest addressable term left, and the only one a grouping could earn its keep on. So I tried to find some root cause of its effect.

Is the rest of it in the weekly shape?

The residual is real. The question is where to look for it, and I tried to do so in the weekly shape — the share of a week falling on each weekday. Shape is worth testing because it is a different axis from volume by construction. But that is also why the link cannot be assumed: the dominant component of the item effect has been removed from the object on purpose, so whether anything of the item effect is left in that object is an open empirical question — and the cheapest one available, because it decides whether a clustering of the shape space is worth building at all.

Could the residual be understood from categorizing items based on their weekly shape? Each week is normalised to 1, the weeks are pooled, and the result is a 7-vector summing to 1, so how much an item sells drops out and only when it sells remains.

The left and right plot reveal these weekly patterns with different perspectives, but they both seem to suggest that we are facing a continuum - which wouldn’t give us any new way to track down the residual error.

In case the construction isn’t clear, I animated it (with manim) on planted archetypes: four invented weekly shapes, scattered and then redrawn as counts at the items’ real weekly totals. It is synthetic data that shows what the space is and what genuine structure in it would look like, which is what makes the measurement below legible either way.

So I regress \(\alpha_i\) on volume, sparsity and the per-item noise level, then add the six free shape coordinates (the sum adds up to one, so the seventh is derived from the first six) and ask what they buy. I defined three rules:

  • Shape is measured on history strictly before the panel’s first origin. A profile computed over the scored windows would be partly measured on what it predicts.
  • Six extra regressors explain variance by construction — about \(k/n\) of what is left. That is removed in expectation, and a permutation test gives the distribution instead of the expectation. The shuffle is stratified by volume, so the null keeps whatever relationship shape has with volume and destroys only its link to difficulty. Permuting freely would break the shape–volume relationship too, make the null easier to beat, and bias the test toward finding an effect.
  • Shape is itself measured with error, and error in a predictor attenuates what it explains toward zero — the mirror image of the attenuation above. So the increment is reported both as measured and divided by the shape’s own reliability, as an upper bound on what a perfectly measured shape could contribute.

Conclusion: the weekly shape carries essentially none of the error. The observed increment is 0.7% of the stable difficulty, below the null’s 95th percentile of 1.5%, at p = 0.15 over 1000 permutations in 10 volume strata. Shape explains less than a typical volume-preserving shuffle does.

And the result is bounded. The profile’s own split-half reliability on these items is 0.44, so a perfectly measured weekly shape could account for at most 1.5% — the fourth bar from the figure. There is no version of this measurement, however much the ruler improves, in which the weekly shape reaches the 15.6% that is still on the item.

The per-weekday correlations on the right never leave ±0.16, so no single weekday carries difficulty either.

Where this leaves the question

What we established about the error’s origin. 42% of the variance in log WQL sits on the identity of the item against 2% on the week, with high week-to-week stability - and even six-month stability. Of that, 63% is volume — a continuous covariate already present in the history, needing no clustering. The 15.6% that remains is item-specific, stable, and not in the weekly shape, at a ceiling of 1.5%. The shape space is calendar-anchored, and its measurability rises monotonically with volume.

Leads that ares till open. Whether that residual is organised at all, and on what axis. Candidates I have not tested are, for example: burstiness and volatility, lifecycle stage, promotion and price response, intermittency structure beyond the zero-day share…

What it means for our model’s performances. We started this post by wondering if we could leverage some hidden structure’s correlation with prediction difficulty. And we did find such structure: however the irony is that it is inherently already accessible and used by Chronos2. Therefore, we are back to the same stage as in the end of the previous post, though our understanding is much greater.

[The previous post](../02-chronos2/) ended flat. One lever was left:
**treat different items differently**. Fine-tune per group, cross-learn inside a group, one
model per group.

That lever has a precondition, and it is testable beforhand.
For a static grouping to help, **difficulty has to be a stable property of an item.** If the
items that are hard this week are not the ones that were hard last week, it means 
the error cannot be decreased by finding a different setup for the worst items, as it is akin to a transient effect.

This post answers that, and then keeps narrowing. Difficulty *is* a stable property of the
item. Two thirds of it is volume, which needs no clustering to exploit.

## The panel

To analyse the difficulty's, we divide the time horizon in twenty-four origins, one week apart, in order to 
score over *7-day non-overlapping windows* (non-overlapping matters: with overlapping windows the correlation between consecutive
origins would be partly an artefact of shared days). The **restricted model used** in this post is the zero-shot one at the
context length chosen for days 1–7 (see [the previous post](../02-chronos2/)), **predicting 7 days ahead only** — the fine-tuned model is disqualified here, because
walking its test-origin checkpoint backwards would let it see the future of every earlier
window.

```{python}
#| label: panel-build

    for origin in ORIGINS
```

Though we can suspect an at least mild correlation between weeks, as the graph suggests some horizontal lines, the error ditribution and origin needs to be properly assessed. So we split the error of each (item, origin), written $x_{i,o}$, cell in **four ways**:

$$x_{i,o} = \mu + \alpha_i + \beta_o + \varepsilon_{i,o}$$

where $\mu$ is the **global mean**; $\alpha_i$ is the **item** — by construction the only part a static
grouping could exploit; $\beta_o$ is the **week** - are some weeks are hard for everyone? What is left
splits further into a part that survives to the next origin (*persistent*), a part that does not (*transient*), and the
sampling *noise* of the cell itself, measured from the odd and even days of each window. It is
all contained within $\varepsilon_{i,o}$.

Our task now is to **scan the distribution for clusters and continuums.**

## Does the estimator work?

Before touching the real panel, I ran our scaning method on **a simulated one with known components**, at
the same panel size and noise level. The results are:

```{python}
#| label: fig-control
#| fig-cap: "Left: planted components against recovered ones, on a simulated panel of the same shape as the real one. Right: the same estimator on a planted drift and nothing else — no constant item effect at all — as the drift's autocorrelation rises. The two together track what was planted; which of the two it lands in does not."
#| code-fold: true
control, planted = simulate_panel(n_series=250, n_origins=N_ORIGINS, seed=SEED)
recovered = decompose(control).shares()
components = ["series", "origin", "persistent", "transient", "noise"]
planted_pct = np.array([100 * planted[c] for c in components])
recovered_pct = np.array([100 * recovered["series_adjusted" if c == "series" else c]
                          for c in components])

# The same estimator, on a panel whose only stable signal is an AR(1) drift.
DRIFT_GRID = [0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.75, 0.85, 0.92, 0.97]
drift = []
for drift_rho in DRIFT_GRID:
    simulated, truth = simulate_panel(n_series=250, n_origins=N_ORIGINS, rho=drift_rho,
                                      sd_series=0.0, sd_persistent=0.33, seed=0)
    split = decompose(simulated).shares()
    drift.append((100 * split["series_adjusted"], 100 * split["persistent"],
                  100 * (truth["series"] + truth["persistent"])))
drift_item, drift_persistent, drift_planted = (np.array(v) for v in zip(*drift))
drift_total_error = float(np.abs(drift_item + drift_persistent - drift_planted).max())

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 3.6))

ax = axes[0]
order = np.argsort(planted_pct)
y = np.arange(len(components))
ax.barh(y + 0.19, planted_pct[order], height=0.36, color=plots.GRID_COLOR, label="planted")
ax.barh(y - 0.19, recovered_pct[order], height=0.36, color=plots.MAIN_COLOR,
        label="recovered")
for row, left, right in zip(y, planted_pct[order], recovered_pct[order]):
    ax.text(max(left, right) + 1.2, row, f"{left:.0f} / {right:.0f}", va="center",
            fontsize=8, color=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR)
ax.set_yticks(y); ax.set_yticklabels([components[i] for i in order])
ax.set_xlim(0, max(planted_pct.max(), recovered_pct.max()) * 1.25)
ax.set_xlabel("share of the variance budget (%)")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, frameon=False, loc="lower right")
ax.set_title("the estimator recovers what was planted", fontsize=9, loc="left",
             color=plots.TEXT_COLOR)

ax = axes[1]
ax.plot(DRIFT_GRID, drift_planted, color=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR, linestyle="--",
        linewidth=1.4, label="planted (item + persistent)")
ax.plot(DRIFT_GRID, drift_item + drift_persistent, color=plots.TEXT_COLOR, marker="o",
        markersize=4, linewidth=1.8, label="recovered, the two together")
ax.plot(DRIFT_GRID, drift_item, color=plots.MAIN_COLOR, marker="o", markersize=4,
        linewidth=1.5, label="recovered as item")
ax.plot(DRIFT_GRID, drift_persistent, color=plots.MAIN_COLOR, marker="o", markersize=4,
        linewidth=1.5, linestyle=":", label="recovered as persistent")
ax.set_xlabel("autocorrelation of the planted drift")
ax.set_ylabel("share of the budget (%)")
ax.set_title("but not which of the two it is", fontsize=9, loc="left",
             color=plots.TEXT_COLOR)
ax.legend(fontsize=7.5, frameon=False, loc="upper left")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The left subfigure proves **we can trust the method** for recovering error shares. However, the right subfigure is the method's one real limit. In case of a *slow drift*, it is hard to tell from a constant difficulty over six months, and the estimator divides it badly: at an
autocorrelation of `{python} f"{DRIFT_GRID[-1]}"`, a drift with *no* constant item effect
planted anywhere comes back as a `{python} f"{drift_item[-1]:.0f}"`% **item** effect. The
total it does get right, to within `{python} f"{drift_total_error:.1f}"` points everywhere on
the grid.

The takeaway is that the item share answers *could a grouping fitted once and left alone help*, and the sum answers 
*is there anything stable at all, even if it has to be refitted*.

## Where the error lives
Now what does our method yield for our real pannel?

```{python}

#| tbl-cap: "The variance budget of log WQL. Intervals from 1,000 resamples of the items — not of the cells, since cells within an item are not independent and that dependence is the thing being measured. CI columns are nonparametric bootstrap percentile intervals over items, at 95%."
#| code-fold: true
intervals = bootstrap_shares(panel, n_draws=1000, seed=SEED)
md(pl.DataFrame([
    {"component": name, "share %": 100 * shares[key],
     "CI low %": 100 * intervals[key][0], "CI high %": 100 * intervals[key][1]}
    for name, key in [("item (raw)", "series"),
                      ("item (bias-adjusted)", "series_adjusted"),
                      ("week", "origin"),
                      ("item x week", "interaction"),
                      ("  - persistent", "persistent"),
                      ("  - transient", "transient"),
                      ("  - sampling noise", "noise"),
                      ("stable (item + persistent)", "stable")]]), digits=1)
```

Two numbers summarise the section. 

1. **The identity of the item accounts for `{python} f"{100 * shares['series_adjusted']:.0f}"`% of the variance in log WQL** once the
noise inside each $\alpha_i$ is removed.
1. **the week accounts for `{python} f"{100 * shares['origin']:.0f}"`%** — there are essentially no weeks that are hard
for everybody. Difficulty mostly comes from the item.

Most of what remains is sampling noise, which is what forecasting 7-day windows of items
selling a few units a week buys you. That matters for the next section, in which we try to findt he same result with a different tool. 

## Two instruments, one answer

The previous section gave us our conclusion. But a decomposition is a model, and **a model can be wrong.** Thereofer, we may want to use something like **rank agreement to confirm our findings**: it is a direct measurement of consistency, with *no assumptions* behind it:

```{python}
#| label: fig-ranks
#| fig-cap: "Left: Spearman correlation of the difficulty ranking between consecutive origins. Right: first origin against last, six months apart."
#| code-fold: true
ranks = rank_agreement(panel, decile=0.10)
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 3.6))
ax = axes[0]
values = ranks["consecutive"]
ax.plot(range(1, len(values) + 1), values, color=plots.MAIN_COLOR, marker="o", markersize=4)
ax.axhline(ranks["consecutive_median"], color=plots.TEXT_COLOR, linestyle="--", linewidth=1)
ax.axhspan(-0.5, 0.5, color=plots.GRID_COLOR, alpha=0.6, zorder=0)
ax.text(0.98, 0.04, "|rho| < 0.5: 'weak' for test-retest", transform=ax.transAxes,
        ha="right", va="bottom", color=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR, fontsize=8)
ax.set_ylim(-1, 1)
ax.set_xlabel("pair of consecutive origins")
ax.set_ylabel("Spearman of the difficulty ranking")
ax = axes[1]
first, last = panel.x[:, 0], panel.x[:, -1]
ax.scatter(first, last, s=10, color=plots.MAIN_COLOR, alpha=0.6)
limits = [min(first.min(), last.min()), max(first.max(), last.max())]
ax.plot(limits, limits, color=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR, linewidth=1)
ax.set_xlabel(f"log WQL at {panel.origins[0]}")
ax.set_ylabel(f"... at {panel.origins[-1]}")
ax.text(0.03, 0.95, f"Spearman {ranks['first_vs_last']:+.2f}", transform=ax.transAxes,
        va="top", color=plots.TEXT_COLOR)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Immediately, the median of `{python} f"{ranks['consecutive_median']:+.2f}"` between consecutive origins
looks like a weak agreement, challenging the previous section's result (test–retest conventions call anything under 0.5 weak). Taken at
face value, the two sections seem to **disagree: a large item effect, and a ranking that only half holds.**

But they don't. The decomposition predicts the rank correlation, and this prediction can be written down from the variance shares alone:

1. Within one origin the week effect is a constant and does not touch the across-items ranking,
so the spread of the measured error at a single origin is
$\operatorname{V}_{cell} = \operatorname{var}(\alpha) + v_{\text{pers}} + v_{\text{trans}} + v_{\text{noise}}$
2. The covariance between two consecutive origins keeps only the parts that survive the step from the previous value,
$\operatorname{var}(\alpha) + v_{\text{pers}}$. 
3. Their ratio is the correlation the model expects anyone to observe. Six months apart the persistent part has decayed and only
$\operatorname{var}(\alpha)$ remains.
4. So rank correlation is equal to $\frac{\operatorname{var}(\alpha)}{\operatorname{V}_{cell}}$.

Therefore **noise** enters only in the denominator, and being responsible for around 40% of cell's error, so **it halves this correlation rate**, despite being unrepresentative of the actual phenomenon.

So divide instead by the **reliable** part of a cell — everything except the sampling noise —
and what comes out is the correlation between the underlying difficulties rather than between
their noisy estimates: 

```{python}
#| label: fig-attenuation
#| fig-cap: "Left: what the decomposition predicts the rank correlation will be, what the rank statistic actually observes, and the correlation between the underlying difficulties once the window's sampling noise is divided out. The grey bar is the attenuation the blunt ruler costs. Right: observed minus predicted, for the two quantities."
#| code-fold: true
from matplotlib.lines import Line2D

v_alpha = shares["series_adjusted"]
v_pers, v_trans, v_noise = shares["persistent"], shares["transient"], shares["noise"]
cell_variance = v_alpha + v_pers + v_trans + v_noise
reliability = (v_alpha + v_pers + v_trans) / cell_variance

attenuation = pl.DataFrame([
    {"quantity": "consecutive origins",
     "predicted": (v_alpha + v_pers) / cell_variance,
     "observed": ranks["consecutive_median"],
     "noise-corrected": (v_alpha + v_pers) / (v_alpha + v_pers + v_trans)},
    {"quantity": "first vs last origin",
     "predicted": v_alpha / cell_variance,
     "observed": ranks["first_vs_last"],
     "noise-corrected": v_alpha / (v_alpha + v_pers + v_trans)},
])

predicted = attenuation["predicted"].to_numpy()
observed = attenuation["observed"].to_numpy()
corrected = attenuation["noise-corrected"].to_numpy()
rows = np.arange(attenuation.height)[::-1]          # first quantity on top

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 2.9),
                         gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1.7, 1]})

ax = axes[0]
for row, p, o, c in zip(rows, predicted, observed, corrected):
    ax.plot([min(p, o), c], [row, row], color=plots.GRID_COLOR, linewidth=7,
            solid_capstyle="round", zorder=1)
    ax.scatter(p, row, s=80, facecolor="white", edgecolor=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR,
               linewidth=1.6, zorder=3)
    ax.scatter(o, row, s=55, color=plots.MAIN_COLOR, zorder=4)
    ax.scatter(c, row, s=80, color=plots.TEXT_COLOR, marker="D", zorder=3)
ax.set_yticks(rows)
ax.set_yticklabels(attenuation["quantity"].to_list())
ax.set_ylim(-0.7, attenuation.height - 0.3)
ax.set_xlim(0, 1)
ax.axvline(0.5, color=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR, linestyle=":", linewidth=1, zorder=0)
ax.text(0.49, -0.62, "0.5: 'weak' for test-retest", ha="right", va="bottom",
        color=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR, fontsize=8)
ax.set_xlabel("Spearman correlation of the difficulty ranking")
ax.legend(handles=[
    Line2D([], [], marker="o", linestyle="", markerfacecolor="white",
           markeredgecolor=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR, markersize=9, label="predicted"),
    Line2D([], [], marker="o", linestyle="", color=plots.MAIN_COLOR,
           markersize=8, label="observed"),
    Line2D([], [], marker="D", linestyle="", color=plots.TEXT_COLOR,
           markersize=8, label="noise-corrected"),
], loc="lower left", bbox_to_anchor=(0, 1.02), ncol=3, frameon=False, fontsize=8)

ax = axes[1]
residual = observed - predicted
span = float(np.abs(residual).max()) * 2.4
ax.barh(rows, residual, color=plots.MAIN_COLOR, height=0.38)
ax.axvline(0, color=plots.TEXT_COLOR, linewidth=1)
for row, value in zip(rows, residual):
    offset = 0.05 * span if value > 0 else -0.05 * span
    ax.text(value + offset, row, f"{value:+.3f}", va="center",
            ha="left" if value > 0 else "right", color=plots.TEXT_COLOR, fontsize=9)
ax.set_yticks(rows); ax.set_yticklabels([]); ax.tick_params(left=False)
ax.set_ylim(-0.7, attenuation.height - 0.3)

plt.show()
```

The figure shows the difference between the original blunt ruler and the more faithfull one: the grey bar is what the bluntness
costs, and the underlying difficulty ordering is stable at `{python} f"{attenuation['noise-corrected'][0]:.2f}"` - **confirming the previous section's strong conclusion.**

The residuals are reassuring (right pannel): they fall on opposite sides of zero. A systematic
error in the variance model would push both the same way.

The six-month row says something further. Nearly all of the long-run agreement is the
$\alpha$ term — the persistent part has decayed by then — so $\alpha$ is **behaving like a genuinely fixed property of the item rather than a slow drift**. If we remember section "Does the estimator work?"'s warning, we see that we are safe.

## Most of it is volume

A large item share isn't the end of our analysis. WQL is scale-free, so a
dependence on volume would not be an artefact of units — it would be a statement about
predictability: *"an item selling a third of a unit a day has an **irreducibly high relative error**, and that is a permanent property of the item"*.

That distinction decides what $\alpha_i$ is worth. If it is a function of volume, then
"group the items by difficulty" means **"bucket them by volume"**, which needs no clustering and
which the forecaster reads off the history unaided.

One correction is mandatory though. The sampling noise of a 7-day window is itself
strongly volume-dependent, so low-volume items have their $\alpha$ **inflated** more — which
would manufacture exactly the relationship I am testing for. A global bias adjustment cannot
fix a bias that differs per item, so I measure each item's noise from its own odd and even
days and remove it individually.

```{python}
#| label: tbl-volume
#| tbl-cap: "What the stable item effect is made of. Each row removes one more thing; the share is of the whole error budget."
#| code-fold: true
volume_record = RECORD["stable_vs_volume"]
rho = volume_record["spearman"]
md(pl.DataFrame([
    {"removed": "nothing (raw item effect)", "Spearman with alpha": None,
     "item share of the budget %": 100 * volume_record["share_raw"]},
    {"removed": "each item's own measured noise", "Spearman with alpha": None,
     "item share of the budget %": 100 * volume_record["share_noise_corrected"]},
    {"removed": "+ log volume", "Spearman with alpha": rho["log volume"],
     "item share of the budget %": None},
    {"removed": "+ zero-day share", "Spearman with alpha": rho["zero-day share"],
     "item share of the budget %": None},
    {"removed": "+ both covariates", "Spearman with alpha": None,
     "item share of the budget %": 100 * volume_record["share_after_covariates"]},
]), digits=2)
```

So we obtain a good linear tendency that confirms the initial statement: volume and sparsity together explain
**`{python} f"{100 * volume_record['r2_of_alpha']:.0f}"`%** of the stable difficulty. Two
details matter for reading that.

**They are not two different readings.** Volume alone reaches the same
`{python} f"{100 * volume_record['r2_of_alpha']:.0f}"`%, soadding the zero-day share moves
nothing. At a Spearman of `{python} f"{rho['zero-day share']:+.2f}"` with $\alpha$ and a
near-mechanical relationship to volume on data that is two thirds zeros, sparsity is a second
view of the same quantity, not an independent factor.

**And the noise channel is not what is driving it.** Wwe already justified that a noisier estimate of the same
underlying error could have a different expectation. The direct test is to ask how much of
$\alpha$ the measurement noise itself explains: it reaches
`{python} f"{abs(rho['noise level (control)']):.2f}"` as a Spearman against
`{python} f"{abs(rho['log volume']):.2f}"` for volume, and 34% of the variance against 63%.
If the volume relationship were an artefact of the noise level, the noise level would be the
proximate cause and the stronger correlate. It is the weaker one, by a clear margin.

So **two thirds of the stable item effect is a sort on a number already in the history**, and
one third is not:
`{python} f"{100 * volume_record['share_after_covariates']:.1f}"`% of the whole error budget,
against `{python} f"{100 * shares['origin']:.1f}"`% for the week and
`{python} f"{100 * (shares['persistent'] + shares['transient']):.1f}"`% for everything
transient. It is the **largest addressable term left**, and the only one a grouping could earn
its keep on. So I tried to find some root cause of its effect.

## Is the rest of it in the weekly shape?

The residual is real. The question is where to look for it, and I tried to do so in the **weekly shape** — the share of a week
falling on each weekday. Shape is worth testing because it is a different axis from volume by construction. But that
is also why the link cannot be assumed: the dominant component of the item effect has been
removed from the object on purpose, so whether anything of the item effect is left in that
object is an open empirical question — and the cheapest one available, because it decides
whether a clustering of the shape space is worth building at all.

**Could the residual be understood from categorizing items based on their weekly shape?** Each week is normalised to 1, the weeks are pooled, and the result is *a 7-vector summing to 1*, so how much an item sells drops out and only *when* it sells remains.

Let's see what that looks like:

So I regress $\alpha_i$ on volume, sparsity and the per-item noise level, then add the six
free shape coordinates (the sum adds up to one, so the seventh is derived from the first six) and ask what they buy. I defined three rules:

- **Shape is measured on history strictly before the panel's first origin.** A profile
  computed over the scored windows would be partly measured on what it predicts.
- **Six extra regressors explain variance by construction** — about $k/n$ of what is left.
  That is removed in expectation, and a **permutation test** gives the distribution instead
  of the expectation. The shuffle is **stratified by volume**, so the null keeps whatever
  relationship shape has with volume and destroys only its link to difficulty. Permuting
  freely would break the shape–volume relationship too, make the null easier to beat, and
  bias the test toward finding an effect.
- **Shape is itself measured with error**, and error in a *predictor* attenuates what it
  explains toward zero — the mirror image of the attenuation above. So the increment is
  reported both as measured and divided by the shape's own reliability, as an upper bound on
  what a perfectly measured shape could contribute.

```{python}
#| label: fig-shape-test
#| fig-cap: "Left: what the weekly shape adds to the stable difficulty, against the volume-stratified permutation null. Right: each weekday's own rank correlation with difficulty, before volume is removed."
#| code-fold: true
bridge = RECORD["shape_vs_alpha"]
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 3.4), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1, 1.15]})

ax = axes[0]
bars = [("observed", bridge["increment"], plots.MAIN_COLOR),
        ("null median", bridge["null_median"], plots.GRID_COLOR),
        ("null 95th pct", bridge["null_p95"], plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR),
        ("ceiling if shape\nwere perfect", bridge["increment_disattenuated"],
         plots.TEXT_COLOR)]
ax.barh([b[0] for b in bars], [100 * b[1] for b in bars],
        color=[b[2] for b in bars], height=0.6)
ax.invert_yaxis()
ax.set_xlabel("share of the stable difficulty that shape explains (%)")
ax.text(0.97, 0.08, f"p = {bridge['p_value']:.2f}", transform=ax.transAxes,
        ha="right", color=plots.TEXT_COLOR)

ax = axes[1]
ax.bar(range(WEEKDAYS), bridge["spearman_per_weekday"], color=plots.MAIN_COLOR, width=0.6)
ax.axhline(0, color=plots.MUTED_TEXT_COLOR, linewidth=1)
ax.set_xticks(range(WEEKDAYS)); ax.set_xticklabels(DAY_NAMES)
ax.set_ylim(-0.4, 0.4)
ax.set_ylabel(r"Spearman with $\alpha_i$")
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

Conclusion: **the weekly shape carries essentially none of the error.** The observed increment is
`{python} f"{100 * bridge['increment']:.1f}"`% of the stable difficulty, *below* the null's
95th percentile of `{python} f"{100 * bridge['null_p95']:.1f}"`%, at
p = `{python} f"{bridge['p_value']:.2f}"` over
`{python} str(bridge['n_permutations'])` permutations in
`{python} str(bridge['n_strata'])` volume strata. Shape explains less than a typical
volume-preserving shuffle does.

And the result is **bounded**. The profile's own split-half reliability on
these items is `{python} f"{bridge['shape_reliability']:.2f}"`, so a perfectly measured
weekly shape could account for at most
`{python} f"{100 * bridge['increment_disattenuated']:.1f}"`% — the fourth bar from the figure. There is
no version of this measurement, however much the ruler improves, in which the weekly shape
reaches the `{python} f"{100 * volume_record['share_after_covariates']:.1f}"`% that is still
on the item.

The per-weekday correlations on the right never leave ±0.16, so no single weekday carries difficulty either.

## Where this leaves the question

**What we established about the error's origin.**
`{python} f"{100 * shares['series_adjusted']:.0f}"`% of the variance in log WQL sits on the
**identity of the item** against `{python} f"{100 * shares['origin']:.0f}"`% on the week, with high week-to-week stability - and even six-month stability. Of that,
`{python} f"{100 * volume_record['r2_of_alpha']:.0f}"`% is **volume** — a continuous covariate
already present in the history, needing no clustering. The
`{python} f"{100 * volume_record['share_after_covariates']:.1f}"`% that remains is
item-specific, stable, and **not** in the weekly shape, at a ceiling of
`{python} f"{100 * bridge['increment_disattenuated']:.1f}"`%. The shape space is
calendar-anchored, and its measurability rises monotonically with volume.

**Leads that ares till open.** Whether that residual is organised at all, and on what axis. Candidates I have not
tested are, for example: burstiness and volatility, lifecycle stage, promotion and price response,
intermittency structure beyond the zero-day share...

**What it means for our model's performances**. We started this post by wondering if we could leverage some hidden structure's correlation with prediction difficulty. And we did find such structure: however the irony is that it is inherently already accessible and used by Chronos2. Therefore, we are back to the same stage as in the end of the previous post, though our understanding is much greater.
